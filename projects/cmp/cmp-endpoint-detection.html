Understanding Endpoint Detection

Endpoint detection is critical for stopping the CMP process at precisely the right moment when the target material layer has been cleared without over-polishing. Multiple sensing modalities provide real-time feedback: motor current monitoring detects changes in friction as different materials are exposed, optical interferometry measures film thickness changes through reflectance, eddy current sensors track metal layer thickness, and temperature sensors detect thermal signature variations. Advanced endpoint systems correlate multiple signals using pattern recognition algorithms to improve reliability and reduce false positives. Accurate endpoint detection maximizes yield, minimizes material waste, and enables tight process control.

Theoretical Background

1. Motor Current Monitoring

Motor current provides an indirect measure of friction torque at the wafer-pad interface. As the polishing process transitions from removing one material to another (e.g., metal to oxide), the friction coefficient changes, causing a corresponding change in motor current. This method is simple and non-invasive but can be affected by pad conditioning, slurry flow variations, and platen speed changes.

I_motor = I_baseline + k × μ × F_N × r × ω

where I is motor current, k is a conversion constant, μ is friction coefficient, F_N is normal force, r is effective radius, and ω is rotational speed.

2. Optical Interferometry

Optical endpoint detection uses light reflection from the wafer surface to monitor film thickness in real-time. Constructive and destructive interference patterns change as the film thickness decreases, creating oscillations in the reflected light intensity. The endpoint is detected when a characteristic reflectance pattern corresponding to the underlying layer is observed.

R = |r₁ + r₂ × exp(i × 4π × n × d / λ)|²

where R is reflectance, r₁ and r₂ are reflection coefficients at interfaces, n is refractive index, d is film thickness, and λ is wavelength.

3. Eddy Current Sensing

Eddy current sensors are particularly useful for detecting endpoints when polishing metal films. An oscillating magnetic field induces eddy currents in the conductive layer, and the sensor measures the impedance change as the metal thickness decreases. When the metal is fully removed, the eddy current signal drops dramatically, providing a clear endpoint signal.

Z = Z₀ × [1 + α × exp(-d / δ)]

where Z is sensor impedance, Z₀ is baseline impedance, α is sensitivity coefficient, d is metal thickness, and δ is skin depth.

4. Temperature Monitoring

Different materials have different thermal properties (thermal conductivity, specific heat), so the transition from one material to another can cause changes in the thermal signature at the pad-wafer interface. Temperature sensors embedded in the pad or carrier can detect these changes. The method is less common but can complement other techniques.

ΔT = Q / (k_eff × A)

where ΔT is temperature change, Q is heat generation rate, k_eff is effective thermal conductivity (material-dependent), and A is contact area.

5. Signal Processing and Filtering

Raw endpoint signals are noisy due to pad asperities, slurry flow variations, and mechanical vibrations. Digital signal processing techniques such as moving average filters, Savitzky-Golay filters, and wavelet transforms are used to extract the underlying trend and reduce false endpoint calls.

S_filtered(t) = (1/N) × Σ[S(t - iΔt)] for i = 0 to N-1

where S_filtered is the filtered signal, N is the filter window size, and Δt is the sampling interval.

6. Derivative-Based Endpoint Detection

Endpoint is often characterized by a rapid change in signal slope rather than an absolute threshold. Taking the first or second derivative of endpoint signals can enhance the transition point, making it easier to detect. A threshold on the derivative magnitude triggers the endpoint call.

dS/dt = [S(t) - S(t - Δt)] / Δt

Endpoint is detected when |dS/dt| exceeds a predefined threshold value.

7. Multi-Signal Correlation

Combining multiple endpoint signals (motor current, optical, eddy current, temperature) through correlation algorithms improves reliability and reduces false positives. Machine learning approaches can weight different signals based on historical data and process conditions to optimize endpoint detection accuracy.

EP_score = w₁×S₁ + w₂×S₂ + w₃×S₃ + w₄×S₄

where EP_score is the endpoint confidence score, w_i are weights, and S_i are normalized signal contributions. Endpoint is called when EP_score exceeds a threshold.

8. Over-Polish Control

After endpoint detection, a controlled over-polish time is often applied to ensure complete clearing across the entire wafer. The over-polish duration is optimized to clear slow-polishing regions (center or edge) without excessive removal in fast-polishing areas. Typical over-polish is 5-20% of the main polish time.

t_overpolish = f × t_endpoint

where t_overpolish is over-polish time, f is over-polish factor (typically 0.05-0.20), and t_endpoint is the time to endpoint detection.
