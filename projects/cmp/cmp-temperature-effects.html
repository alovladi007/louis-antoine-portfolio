Understanding Temperature Effects in CMP

Temperature plays a critical role in CMP performance through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Frictional heating at the wafer-pad interface generates thermal gradients that affect slurry rheology, chemical reaction rates, pad mechanical properties, and material removal uniformity. Temperature rise is influenced by friction coefficient, relative velocity, applied pressure, and heat dissipation rates. Understanding these thermal effects is essential for maintaining process stability, especially for advanced nodes where even small temperature variations can impact critical dimension control and within-wafer uniformity.

Theoretical Background

1. Frictional Heat Generation

Heat generation in CMP arises primarily from friction between the wafer and polishing pad. The frictional power dissipated is proportional to the normal force, friction coefficient, and relative sliding velocity. This heat flux is distributed between the wafer, pad, and slurry, with partitioning determined by thermal properties and contact conditions.

Q = μ × F_N × V

where Q is heat generation rate (W), μ is friction coefficient, F_N is normal force (N), and V is relative velocity (m/s).

2. Temperature Distribution Model

The temperature distribution across the wafer is governed by the heat equation with frictional source terms and boundary conditions representing cooling at the backside and edges. In steady state, the radial temperature profile can be approximated analytically for axisymmetric loading.

T(r) = T_edge + (Q / 4πk) × [1 - (r/R)²]

where T(r) is temperature at radius r, k is thermal conductivity, and R is wafer radius.

3. Slurry Viscosity Temperature Dependence

Slurry viscosity decreases exponentially with temperature following an Arrhenius-type relationship. Lower viscosity improves transport and slurry penetration into the pad-wafer interface but may reduce removal rates if viscous drag is beneficial for particle transport.

η(T) = η_0 × exp[E_a / (R × T)]

where η is viscosity (cP), η_0 is reference viscosity, E_a is activation energy, R is gas constant, and T is absolute temperature (K).

4. Arrhenius Kinetics for Chemical Reactions

Chemical reaction rates in CMP follow Arrhenius behavior, with rate constants increasing exponentially with temperature. For oxidation-based removal mechanisms, higher temperatures accelerate surface layer formation and dissolution, potentially increasing removal rates.

k(T) = A × exp[-E_a / (R × T)]

where k is reaction rate constant, A is pre-exponential factor, and E_a is activation energy (typically 20-60 kJ/mol for CMP chemistries).

5. Pad Stiffness Temperature Dependency

Polishing pads are typically made from polyurethane, which exhibits temperature-dependent mechanical properties. As temperature increases, the elastic modulus decreases and the pad becomes more compliant, affecting contact mechanics and pressure distribution.

E(T) = E_0 × [1 - α × (T - T_0)]

where E is elastic modulus, E_0 is reference modulus, α is temperature coefficient (typically 0.01-0.03 /°C), and T_0 is reference temperature.

6. Hot Spot Formation

Hot spots are localized regions of elevated temperature that can occur due to non-uniform pressure distribution, edge effects, or pattern-dependent contact variations. Hot spots can lead to non-uniform removal rates and process instabilities. They are more pronounced at higher velocities and pressures.

ΔT_hotspot = f × (P_local / P_avg) × ΔT_avg

where f is an enhancement factor (typically 1.5-2.5), P_local is local pressure, and ΔT_avg is average temperature rise.

7. Thermal Equilibrium Time

The time required to reach thermal steady-state depends on the thermal mass and heat transfer characteristics of the system. Typical equilibration times range from 10-60 seconds, meaning that transients during the initial phase of polishing can affect within-wafer uniformity.

τ_thermal = ρ × c_p × L² / k

where τ is thermal time constant, ρ is density, c_p is specific heat, L is characteristic length, and k is thermal conductivity.

8. Cooling Strategies

Active cooling methods include backside wafer cooling, slurry cooling, and platen cooling. Backside cooling via the carrier is most effective for controlling wafer temperature, while slurry flow provides distributed cooling and debris removal. Optimal cooling balances temperature control with process requirements.

Q_cooling = h × A × (T_surface - T_coolant)

where h is heat transfer coefficient (W/m²·K), A is contact area, and T values are surface and coolant temperatures.
