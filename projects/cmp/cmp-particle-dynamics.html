Understanding Particle Dynamics in CMP

Abrasive particles in CMP slurry play a critical role in material removal through mechanical interactions at the wafer-pad interface. Particle dynamics encompass transport through the slurry film, mechanical contact with the wafer surface, force transmission through particle-particle and particle-surface interactions, and agglomeration behavior. The Discrete Element Method (DEM) provides a powerful framework for simulating individual particle trajectories and contact forces. Understanding particle size distributions, particle-surface interactions, and agglomeration kinetics is essential for optimizing slurry formulations and predicting process performance.

Theoretical Background

1. Discrete Element Method (DEM) Fundamentals

DEM is a computational technique that simulates the motion and interaction of individual particles by solving Newton's equations of motion for each particle. Forces on each particle include contact forces (normal and tangential), drag forces from the fluid, gravitational forces, and external applied forces. DEM provides detailed information about particle trajectories, contact forces, and collective behavior.

m_i × d²x_i/dt² = ΣF_contact + F_drag + F_gravity + F_external

where m_i is particle mass, x_i is position, and F terms represent different force contributions.

2. Contact Force Model: Hertzian Contact

Normal contact forces between elastic spheres follow Hertzian contact mechanics, where the force is proportional to the overlap raised to the 3/2 power. This nonlinear relationship captures the elastic deformation of particles during collision. A damping term is added to account for energy dissipation.

F_n = k_n × δ^(3/2) + γ_n × v_n

where k_n is normal stiffness, δ is overlap distance, γ_n is damping coefficient, and v_n is normal relative velocity.

3. Tangential Friction Forces

Tangential forces arise from friction when particles slide against each other or against surfaces. The tangential force is limited by the Coulomb friction criterion, where the maximum static friction is proportional to the normal force.

F_t = min(k_t × δ_t, μ × F_n)

where k_t is tangential stiffness, δ_t is tangential displacement, and μ is friction coefficient.

4. Drag Force in Viscous Fluid

Particles experience drag forces as they move through the slurry. For small particles in the Stokes regime (low Reynolds number), the drag force is proportional to particle velocity relative to the fluid. This is the dominant force for nanoscale abrasive particles.

F_drag = 6π × η × r × (v_fluid - v_particle)

where η is fluid viscosity, r is particle radius, and v terms are velocities.

5. Particle Size Distribution Effects

Real slurries contain particles with a distribution of sizes, often approximated by a log-normal distribution. Particle size affects contact mechanics (larger particles exert higher forces), transport behavior (smaller particles follow fluid streamlines better), and removal efficiency. Optimal distributions balance mechanical abrasion with uniform transport.

f(d) = (1 / (d × σ × √(2π))) × exp[-(ln(d) - μ)² / (2σ²)]

where d is particle diameter, μ is mean of ln(d), and σ is standard deviation of ln(d).

6. Agglomeration Mechanisms

Particles can agglomerate due to van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions, or mechanical entrapment. Agglomeration reduces the effective number of active particles and can lead to defects (scratches from large agglomerates) or reduced removal rates. Surfactants and pH control are used to prevent agglomeration by maintaining repulsive forces.

F_vdW = -A × r / (12 × h²)

where A is Hamaker constant, r is particle radius, and h is surface separation distance.

7. Particle-Surface Indentation and Material Removal

When particles contact the wafer surface under load, they indent and remove material through plastic deformation and fracture. The indentation depth depends on particle hardness, applied load, and contact geometry. Material removal per particle per pass is proportional to the indented volume.

V_indent = k × (F / H_wafer)^(3/2) × r^(1/2)

where V_indent is indented volume, F is contact force, H_wafer is wafer hardness, and r is particle radius.

8. Shear-Induced Particle Migration

In shear flows, particles migrate laterally due to gradients in shear rate and particle interactions. This phenomenon, known as shear-induced migration, causes particles to concentrate in certain regions of the flow field. In CMP, this affects the local particle concentration at the wafer-pad interface.

v_migration = -D × ∇φ - φ × v_shear × (∇γ / γ)

where D is diffusion coefficient, φ is particle concentration, γ is shear rate, and ∇ represents gradient.

9. Collision Frequency and Contact Statistics

The frequency of particle-particle and particle-surface collisions depends on particle concentration, relative velocities, and system geometry. Higher collision rates increase the probability of agglomeration but also enhance mixing and material removal. DEM simulations provide detailed contact statistics.

f_collision = 4 × n × r² × v_rel × (1 + φ)

where n is number density, r is particle radius, v_rel is relative velocity, and φ accounts for particle concentration effects.
