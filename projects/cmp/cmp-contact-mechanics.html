About Contact Mechanics in CMP

The Greenwood-Williamson (GW) model treats rough surfaces as collections of spherical asperities with Gaussian height distribution. Real contact area is typically only 1-5% of nominal area, creating localized high pressures that drive material removal. Pad asperity density (10⁷-10⁹ /cm²), mean asperity radius (10-50 μm), and roughness (σ = 5-20 μm) control contact behavior.

Greenwood-Williamson Contact Theory

Asperity Contact Model

The GW model assumes surface roughness consists of spherical asperities with heights following a Gaussian distribution. When two surfaces approach, only asperities above a critical separation d make contact.

φ(z) = (1/√(2πσ)) × exp(-z²/(2σ²))

Real Contact Area

The real contact area Areal is the sum of individual asperity contact areas:

Areal = η × Anom × ∫[d,∞] πR(z-d) φ(z) dz

Where:

  • η = asperity density (number per unit area)
  • R = mean asperity radius
  • d = separation distance
  • φ(z) = height probability density

Contact Pressure

Individual asperity contact pressure depends on elastic deformation (Hertzian contact):

Pasperity = (4/3) × E* × √(R × δ³)

Where E* = E/(1-ν²) is the effective elastic modulus, E is Young's modulus, ν is Poisson's ratio, and δ is asperity deformation.

Load-Area Relationship

For elastic contact, the GW model predicts real contact area proportional to applied load:

Areal ∝ P2/3

This sublinear relationship means contact area grows slowly with pressure, maintaining high local pressures even at moderate nominal loads.

Implications for CMP

  • Real contact area is 1-5% of nominal area → High local pressures (10-100× nominal)
  • Contact spots are discrete and dynamic → Slurry transport between contacts critical
  • Pad conditioning regenerates asperities → Maintains consistent contact mechanics
  • Particle size must match asperity spacing → 50-150 nm particles optimal
  • Soft pads → More contact area, lower local pressure → Better for soft metals
  • Hard pads → Less contact area, higher local pressure → Better for hard oxides
